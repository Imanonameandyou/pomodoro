## BODY COPY

[VISUAL: hero — wide shot of the room. Orange walls, grotto-blue accents, full tables, evening light. Not a plate shot. The room and the people first.]

I've eaten at a lot of restaurants around Orlando that describe themselves as authentic Italian.

Most of them mean the sauce is red.

The word has been used so many times around here that it's become furniture. Every menu says it. Every window decal says it. And then you sit down and get the same gluey alfredo you'd get at a chain off I-4, served by a nineteen-year-old named Brandon who has never heard of Campania.

So here's a more useful question than "is it authentic."

**What would a restaurant have to actually do, at real expense, to be Italian instead of Italian-themed?**

Six things. All six are checkable. All six are sitting inside one building in Flamingo Crossings, five minutes from Disney's western gate.

---

### 1. The staff were born on Italian soil.

[VISUAL: server mid-conversation at a table, laughing, name pin visible.]

Look at a server's name pin at Simply Capri. Under the name, there's a city. Torino. Salerno. Napoli.

They're not decorations. The restaurant runs a formal cultural exchange program and brings its people over from Italy.

You feel it about four minutes after you sit down. Somebody comes over who is genuinely happy you came, wants to know if it's your first time, and has excellent dish recommendations.

Read the reviews and you'll notice something strange: people don't remember the pasta. They remember Antonio. They remember Josephine. There's a guy from Turin named Tony who has been personally named in more reviews than most dishes on the menu.

That's what an Italian restaurant is supposed to do to you.

---

### 2. The oven came from Naples. On a boat.

[VISUAL: the Acunto oven, flame visible, pizzaiolo mid-launch.]

This is the part that's genuinely hard to fake, because it costs a fortune and you can't hide it in the kitchen.

The oven at Simply Capri is a hand-built Acunto, made in Naples, shipped to Florida, and reassembled on site. It runs around 800 degrees. A pizza is in and out in roughly ninety seconds, which is the only way you get that blistered, leopard-spotted underside on a Neapolitan crust.

You cannot get that out of a conveyor oven, neither out of a stone deck at 550.

Nobody buys that oven to look Italian. You buy it because you care what the crust does.

---

### 3. It's not "Italian." It's about thirty miles of Italian coastline.

[VISUAL: menu close-up showing the Amalfitana / Anacapri / Nerano dish names.]

Here's the tell that most people miss.

Italy isn't a cuisine. It's twenty regions that have been arguing about food for six hundred years. A restaurant that serves you Bolognese from the north, carbonara from Rome, and cannoli from Sicily on the same page isn't cooking Italian food. It's cooking a greatest-hits album.

Simply Capri picked a coastline and stayed on it. Capri, Sorrento, the Amalfi Coast, Naples.

You can see it in the names if you know what you're looking at. 

Tagliatelle alla Nerano is from a village of about four hundred people on the Sorrentine peninsula. 

Scialatielli dei Faraglioni is named after the rock formations off Capri. Pizza Anacapri. Insalata di Capri. Scampi Amalfitani.

Those aren't dish names you invent in a branding meeting in Orlando. Those are the names of places somebody actually misses.

---

### 4. The lemon cake is from a bakery in a town of three thousand people.

[VISUAL: delizia al limone, plated, close.]

The Delizia al Limone on the dessert menu is a limoncello sponge cake wrapped in lemon cream. It's made to the recipe from Pasticceria Sal De Riso in Minori, which is a small town on the Amalfi Coast where that cake is a minor religion.

The espresso is Kimbo, imported from Naples.

The mozzarella is imported. Bufala, fior di latte, burrata, all three available as a tasting. The tomatoes on the pizza are San Marzano. The gelato comes from Vivoli, the Florence-founded shop over at Disney Springs.

None of that shows up on a sign out front. It shows up on the invoice. Which is exactly why it's worth paying attention to.

---

### 5. The man who built it also built the Italian restaurants inside EPCOT.

[VISUAL: exterior at dusk, violet flowers at the entrance, cars parked out front.]

Simply Capri is a Nick Valenti concept. Valenti is a James Beard Award winner, chairman emeritus at the Culinary Institute of America, and the former CEO of Patina Restaurant Group. He's the person behind Via Napoli and Tutto Italia at EPCOT.

So when you eat here, you're eating from the same mind that built the Italian food people fly across the country to try inside a theme park.

Except.

No ticket. No parking fee. No monorail. No forty-five minute walk to the back of a park in August with a stroller. You drive up, you park in front of the door for free, and you walk in.

One reviewer on Tripadvisor worked this out on his own and wrote it up better than any ad ever could. His point was that the pizza tastes like the pizza in the park, and getting to it costs about two hundred dollars less.

---

### 6. The plates are smaller than you expect. That's on purpose, and it changes how you order.

[VISUAL: the table shot. Antipasto board, pizza, a pasta, three sets of hands reaching in.]

If you like eating a lot of the same thing, this might not be for you.

If you come in expecting an American-Italian portion, the mountain of penne with a ladle of cream sauce, you will look at your plate and think it's small. Some people have said exactly that in reviews, and they're not wrong about what they saw.

But that's what Southern Italian food is. Simple, modest, four or five ingredients doing one thing well. A Margherita at Simply Capri has San Marzano tomato, mozzarella, Parmigiano and basil on it. That's the whole list. There's nowhere to hide.

So order the way an Italian family would. Get the antipasto board for the table. Share a pizza. Split a pasta. Get one of the fish. Order dessert like you mean it.

Do it that way and you'll be at the table for two hours and leave full. Order it like a chain restaurant and you'll be disappointed by both the portion and the check.

Fair warning, and the only one you'll need.

## BUILD NOTES

**Why these six and not others.** Each point is a different category of proof, so nothing repeats: people (1), equipment (2), geography (3), sourcing (4), pedigree and access (5), honest concession (6). If you swap one out, swap in a different category, don't add a second sourcing point.

**Point 6 is doing more work than it looks like.** It handles the two complaints that show up most in the review corpus (portions and price) before the reader ever sees a menu, it reframes small plates as restraint rather than stinginess, and it raises the average check by teaching people to order for the table. It's also the only honest concession in the piece, which is what makes the other five believable.

**What's deliberately not in here:**
- Any version of "without breaking the bank." The menu contradicts it and it manufactures check shock.
- Any wine count. The list is closer to 50 or 60 labels, not the 100+ figure in the notes.
- "Generous portions" or anything implying volume.
- Date-night framing. This is a table-for-five restaurant.

**Verify before this goes live:**
1. The Salerno chef. That came from a server's comment to a critic in 2024. Confirm the current head chef's hometown, or soften point 3's opener to "the kitchen is run by Italians."
2. The oven temperature and cook time. Confirm with the pizzaiolo. If the dough ferment is 48 or 72 hours, get that number and add it to point 2, it's stronger than the temperature.
3. That the cultural exchange program is still actively running and staffing the floor.
4. Free parking, and whether Sunday Supper is running year-round or seasonally.

**Traffic note:** this is indirect, so feed it with native-style statics and long story copy, not offer-heavy creative. Route: static → this listicle → reservations. And point the media spend at weekday dinner and Sunday, since Saturday at seven is already full and the service quality drops when it is.
